Learn Asking the Right Questions as a Data Analyst with Phoebe · Session 4 of 6

Teaching while you ask

Every open question on your sheet has a second sentence: why you are asking, said out loud. Session 1 showed what it does, trust up and one true thing learned. Tonight is about the sentence itself, because the version most analysts write is true and useless. "The refresh cadence sets the latency budget" is a correct principle that teaches Adeline nothing, since three of its words are words she would have to ask about, and she will not ask. She will nod. The work of this session is making twelve second sentences plain enough that a person who runs pharmacies learns one real thing from each, without anyone giving a lecture.

Part 1 · covers why you cannot hear your own jargon

Your curse of knowledge, not hers

5 min live

In 1989 Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber showed that better-informed people cannot fully set their information aside when predicting what less-informed people will understand, and named it the curse of knowledge. The Tech Writing course teaches it in depth, with what it does to documentation. The analyst's version is smaller and sharper: a teach-back line written in the analyst's own words. "The refresh cadence sets the latency budget" is tapping. The analyst hears the whole tune, cadence, latency, extract, and judges the line clear from inside that experience. Adeline hears three knocks and a word she recognises.

LiveThe words she would have to ask about3 min▶

Take any teach-back line on your sheet and read it aloud to someone who does not do data work. Any word that makes them say "sorry, what do you mean?" is a jargon cost, and session 3's bench prices it exactly: the question takes five minutes instead of three, because you have to explain, and it costs a point of trust, because she is a little less sure you understand her shop. The line was meant to earn trust. With one wrong word it spends it.

The usual offenders, with the plain word that replaces each. Pipeline: the steps a number goes through from the till to the report. Extract: the file we pull out of the system. Schema: the list of columns and what each one means. Latency: how old the number is by the time you see it. Granularity: per outlet, per product, or per sale, whichever is the smallest thing we count. Join: matching two files on something they share, like a product code. Warehouse: the one place all the copies are kept. SLA: the promise about when it arrives. None of the plain versions is longer than a breath, and every one of them is a thing she already knows exists.

The test is not "would a smart person understand this". Adeline is a smart person. The test is whether the word is one she uses at work, and the honest way to find out is her face, which is why the reading is done aloud and never on paper.

Self-studyWhy plain is not dumbed down2 min▶

Douglas Hubbard's definition of measurement, a quantitatively expressed reduction of uncertainty based on one or more observations, is itself a plain sentence. It contains no term of art. It is the most-quoted line in a book about measuring the things people say cannot be measured, and a finance director can check it against her own experience without a glossary. Precision and plainness are not in tension; the words that sound precise to an analyst are usually just familiar.

There is a professional reason plain is the norm and not a courtesy. The person paying for the work has to be able to check it. A teach-back line she cannot decode is a claim she cannot test, and a claim she cannot test is one she either accepts on faith or quietly ignores. Both are bad for you: the first because faith runs out on the first wrong number, the second because you have spent a sentence and taught nothing. Plain words are how a data person makes their reasoning inspectable by the person who has to sign it off.

Real world

The nod that cost six weeks. A constructed case, and a common shape. An analyst told Adeline "we will need to denormalise the stock feed", watched her nod, and moved on. Six weeks later, reviewing the first cut of the report, she asked when the stock numbers were coming back. She had taken "denormalise" to mean the stock figures would be removed for now, and had been waiting politely for them to return. Nobody lied and nobody was careless. A word the analyst could not hear as jargon went past a person too polite to stop the meeting, and the belief it left behind was not visible until a build had been made on top of it.

Part 2 · covers the one idea that gets explained properly

One concept per meeting

5 min live

Twelve teach-back lines, and she will remember one. That is not a failure of the other eleven; single sentences land as background, and background is what most of them should be. But one idea per meeting deserves ninety seconds of proper explanation: what it is, why it costs, what it means for her Monday. The choice is not the idea you find most interesting. It is the idea a false belief would attach to if you left it unexplained, because that belief is the one that turns into a wrong build.

LiveChoosing the one2 min▶

Look at the five false beliefs the bench can record on the fast run, because each one is a concept that was never explained. "Needs a daily refresh" attaches to freshness. "Stock levels come from the POS" attaches to typed versus recorded. "Product-level detail for the area managers" attaches to the smallest thing you count. "A dashboard is the deliverable" attaches to tool versus outcome. "Everyone reads it on a laptop" attaches to the device deciding the design. Pick the belief most likely to form in this meeting with this person, and the concept behind it is your one.

For Harbourline there are two honest candidates. Freshness is a cost, weekly is a choice, because "you'd want this daily, right?" is the belief waiting to happen the moment anyone says the report is late, and it is the costliest habit in the bank once built. Or typed versus recorded, because she assumes stock is in the POS, it is in a Friday spreadsheet with three versions, and every stock number in the build inherits that. Either is right. Choose one, not both; two concepts in ninety seconds each is a lecture with an interval.

LiveThe ninety-second version2 min▶

Three sentences, in a fixed order: what it is, why it costs, what it means for her Monday. Written in full for the freshness concept, as it would be said after "if the numbers arrived a day earlier, what would you do differently?" and her answer of "honestly, nothing":

What it is. "How often a number is remade is a choice we make, not something the computer decides; the report can be rebuilt every day, every week, or every month, and the number is only as fresh as the last rebuild." Why it costs. "Every rebuild is an export, a check that nothing broke, and a person to do the checking, so a daily number is five times the work of a weekly one and five times the chances of a wrong number slipping through." Her Monday. "Your reorder is due Tuesday noon and the area managers decide once a week, so a weekly number that is right on Monday morning is the correct one, and I would rather spend the saved time making it trustworthy than making it daily."

Ninety seconds, then back to the next question. The tell that it worked is that she uses the word "weekly" as a choice later in the meeting, rather than as an apology.

Self-studyThe test that she could repeat it to her boss2 min▶

The regional GM asks Adeline on Tuesday what the data person said. If the one concept landed, she can say something like "the report is weekly on purpose; daily would cost five times the work and nobody decides daily." That sentence is the whole test: it is hers, it is short, it carries the reason, and it would survive a sceptical follow-up. If instead she says "they talked about refresh rates", the concept was heard and not learned.

Write the sentence you hope she says before the meeting. It forces the ninety seconds to be built backwards from a line a non-data person could carry, and it is the cheapest rehearsal there is. It also tells you something about the choice itself: if you cannot write a one-line version a GM would repeat, you have picked an idea that matters to you and not to her.

Real world

The wrong one. Constructed. An analyst chose "the product master is the naming problem before the data problem" as the concept for Adeline's meeting, explained it well, and she repeated it perfectly. Two weeks later the first cut of the report was daily, because nobody had said out loud that weekly was a choice, and "you'd want this daily" had gone unchallenged in minute six. The concept she learned was true. It was the wrong one for that meeting, because no false belief was waiting to attach to it.

Part 3 · covers the difference between teaching and talking

Lesson, not lecture

4 min live

A teach-back line passes three tests: plain, true, one sentence. The third is the one analysts fail, because the moment a line gets interesting it wants a second sentence, and then a third. The tell of a lecture is easy to hear from across the room: she stops answering and starts nodding. Rob Fitzpatrick's third rule for talking to customers is the whole discipline in five words, talk less and listen more, and it applies with extra force to the person who is supposedly asking the questions.

LivePlain, true, one sentence2 min▶

Failing, before "who can log in to the POS back office and pull an export?": "Access provisioning is typically the critical-path dependency on data projects because credential ownership sits with IT, and if the admin account is a single point of failure we would want to get a service account issued before any ETL is scoped." True, and it fails all three tests at once: four words she would ask about, two claims joined into one, and a second sentence hiding inside the first.

Passing, same question: "Access is the first blocker on most data projects, so I ask early and I ask by name." Plain, because every word is one she uses; true, because it is a real principle of how these projects fail; one sentence, so she is answering again within three seconds. The failing version would have earned a nod. The passing version earned the name Kenneth, and later, once trust had been built, the fact that he is leaving in January.

Self-studyWhen she asks a follow-up, you have a lesson2 min▶

There is only one proof that a line taught something, and it is a follow-up question from her. "So if we went daily, who would do the checking?" means freshness landed as a cost. "Wait, so the stock file is not from the till?" means typed versus recorded landed. A nod proves nothing, and a polite "makes sense" proves slightly less than nothing, because it closes the topic. Silence after a teach-back line is a reason to ask your next question, not to add a sentence.

On the bench this is the mechanism, not a metaphor. Every open question earns a point of trust, and the sensitive facts on her sheet sit behind trust gates she will not cross unprompted. The teach-back line is where that point is earned: she answers the next question more fully because the last one came with a reason. A lecture spends minutes and earns nothing, which is the same arithmetic as the jargon opener in session 3 with a friendlier face.

Real world

The follow-up that changed the build. Constructed. After "a number typed by a person on a Friday behaves differently from one a till records, and I need to know which I am dealing with", Adeline asked: "so the Friday stock sheet is the weak one, not the sales?" That question was the lesson landing and the project changing shape in the same breath. It moved the first piece of work from a sales chart to a single owned stock file, which is where session 5 will find it.

Build-along

Rewrite your twelve lines for Adeline

★ 22 min · everyone writes

The sheet from session 1, teach-back column only. Twelve lines go in as you wrote them and come out with every word she would have to ask about replaced, still one sentence each. Then one is chosen and given its ninety seconds. If you did not bring a sheet, the twelve lines from the bank are printed in step 1 and they need the same work; several of them were written by an analyst too.

Paste your twelve teach-back lines as they are. No editing yet. If you have no sheet, these twelve are the bank's, two per door in door order, and they are the raw material for the rest of the steps.

★ Step 1 · the twelve, as written1 A dashboard is a tool. I need the outcome, because I can check a tool against an outcome and not the other way round. 2 Whatever you are measured on is the number I should be able to move. Everything else is nice to have. 3 I start with the Monday because a report is only worth what someone does after reading it. 4 The deadline tells me how fresh the data has to be. Fresher costs more, so I only build fresh where a decision needs it. 5 The current file is the spec. Every column in it is a decision somebody once made, and I would rather inherit those than guess. 6 A number that is typed by a person on a Friday behaves differently from one a till records. I need to know which I am dealing with. 7 Every hand-off between systems is a place a number can change. I am counting the hand-offs. 8 Two outlets calling the same thing by two names is the most common reason a total is wrong. Replace each circled word, keeping one sentence. The replacement is the plain thing the word stands for, and it is allowed to be longer than the word it replaces. It is not allowed to become a second sentence.

★ Step 3 · the replacements5 The file you build every Monday is the plan nobody wrote down; every column is a decision someone once made, and I would rather inherit those than guess. 7 Every time a number is copied from one system to another it can change, so I am counting the copies. 10 A table that needs a laptop is not read on a phone, so where they read it decides how it looks.

Read all twelve aloud and cut any that became two. The test is a breath: if you had to take one in the middle, it is two sentences wearing a semicolon. Cut the second half. If the second half was the important half, keep that and cut the first.

Homework

Before session 5

30 min

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What does the curse of knowledge predict about an analyst's own teach-back lines, and why is reading them aloud the test?

Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber's finding is that the better-informed person cannot fully set their knowledge aside when predicting what the less-informed person understands. "Cadence" and "latency" are invisible to the person who uses them daily. The listener's "sorry, what do you mean?" is the only reliable detector, and she will usually nod instead, which is why you read to a colleague first.

2 · Why one concept per meeting, and how is it chosen?

For Harbourline the candidates are freshness ("you'd want this daily, right?") and typed versus recorded (she assumes stock is in the POS). Both are chosen by the belief they prevent, not by how interesting they are. An analyst who picks the naming problem and explains it beautifully can still ship a daily report.

3 · What separates a lesson from a lecture, and what is the only proof that a line taught something?

Fitzpatrick's third rule, talk less and listen more, is the whole discipline. On the bench the mechanism is trust: every open question earns a point, the sensitive facts sit behind trust gates, and the teach-back line is where the point is earned. A lecture spends minutes and earns nothing, the same arithmetic as the jargon opener with a friendlier face.
